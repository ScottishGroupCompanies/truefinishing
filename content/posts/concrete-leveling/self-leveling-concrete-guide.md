## What Is Self-Leveling Concrete?

Self-leveling concrete, often called self-leveling underlayment or SLU, is a polymer-modified cementitious material mixed with water to a pourable consistency. Once poured, it flows and finds its own level with minimal troweling. Most products allow light foot traffic within a few hours and flooring installation within a day or two, depending on thickness and the finished floor.

It is not the same as structural concrete. SLU is designed to correct flatness and smoothness, not to carry structural loads or fix a slab that is actively moving.

### Underlayment vs. topping

-   **Underlayments** are covered by another floor: tile, LVP, carpet, wood or a coating.
-   **Self-leveling toppings** are designed to be the wear surface, sometimes polished or sealed.

## When Does a Floor Need Self-Leveling?

Flooring manufacturers set flatness tolerances. When a slab falls outside them, self-leveler is often the fastest way to fix it.

-   Large-format tile, which needs very flat floors to avoid lippage
-   Click-lock vinyl plank or laminate over dips and humps
-   Old slabs with worn traffic lanes or removed tile mortar ridges
-   Additions where new and old slabs meet at different heights
-   Commercial spaces before resilient flooring or thin coatings

## How Self-Leveling Concrete Is Installed

1.  **Assess and test.** Measure flatness, check moisture and identify cracks or movement.
2.  **Prepare the surface.** Grind to remove contaminants, adhesives and weak paste. See our [floor preparation](/floor-preparation/) process.
3.  **Repair and dam.** Fill cracks, block drains and openings, and set dams at edges.
4.  **Prime.** Apply the manufacturer’s primer so the leveler bonds and does not lose water into the slab.
5.  **Mix and pour.** Mix to exact water ratios and pour continuously to avoid cold joints.
6.  **Spread and smooth.** Use a gauge rake and spiked roller to set depth and release air.
7.  **Cure and verify.** Protect from drafts and direct sun, then check flatness before flooring.

## Common Mistakes That Cause Failures

### Skipping primer

Unprimed concrete can pull water out of the leveler too quickly, causing pinholes, weak surfaces and poor bond.

### Too much water

Adding extra water makes the material flow easier but weakens it and can cause dusting, shrinkage cracks and segregation.

### Pouring over moisture problems

Most underlayments are not moisture barriers. High slab moisture can damage adhesives above the leveler. Testing with methods like [\>ASTM F2170](https://www.astm.org/f2170-19a.html) comes first.

### Ignoring cracks and joints

Moving joints will reflect through the leveler. They must be honored or treated per the manufacturer’s instructions.

## Self-Leveler vs. Grinding vs. Patching

Not every uneven floor needs a full pour. Grinding high spots works when the problem is a few ridges. Patching works for isolated dips. Self-leveling is best when unevenness is widespread or when you need a uniformly smooth surface for large tile or resilient flooring.

The [\>American Concrete Institute](https://www.concrete.org/) defines floor flatness measurement methods used on commercial projects, which many specifiers reference when setting tolerances.

## Planning Around Your Schedule

Self-leveling is often on the critical path between demolition and new flooring. Most products accept tile within a day and moisture-sensitive flooring after the manufacturer drying time, which varies with thickness, temperature and humidity. Northern Colorado winters slow curing, so heated spaces help keep schedules on track.

### Coordinating with other trades

On remodels and tenant finishes, the leveling crew sits between demolition and the flooring installer. Clear communication avoids delays and finger-pointing:

-   Share the flooring product and its flatness and moisture requirements before the pour.
-   Confirm finished floor heights at doors, transitions and appliances so nothing ends up too high.
-   Keep other trades off the pour during curing, including painters and electricians rolling carts.
-   Document flatness readings after cure so the installer knows the floor is within tolerance.

### Temperature and airflow during curing

Most self-leveling products list a minimum and maximum substrate and air temperature. Drafts from open doors and direct sunlight through windows can make the surface dry too quickly and crack, while very cold slabs slow strength gain. In Northern Colorado, that often means closing the space, controlling heat, and keeping fans from blowing directly on fresh material during the first hours.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does self leveling concrete cost per square foot?

As a typical Front Range estimate for 2026, professional self-leveling runs about $3 to $8 per square foot, including grinding and primer. Thin skims are at the low end, while deep fills or slope corrections cost more because material use rises quickly with depth. Measuring the floor first gives a reliable price.

### How thick can self leveling concrete be poured?

It depends on the product. Many self-leveling underlayments can be poured from a feather edge up to about 1 to 1.5 inches in one lift, and some allow deeper pours when extended with aggregate. Always follow the manufacturer limits, since exceeding them can cause cracking and long drying times.

### Can you put self leveling concrete over old concrete?

Yes. Old concrete is the most common substrate. It must be mechanically cleaned to remove adhesives, sealers, paint and weak surface paste, then primed with the manufacturer primer. Cracks and joints are treated first, and moisture is tested so the leveler and the flooring above it perform properly.

### How long does self leveling concrete take to dry?

Many products accept foot traffic in two to four hours and tile within about a day. Moisture-sensitive flooring such as vinyl or wood often needs longer drying, based on thickness and conditions. Cold temperatures and high humidity slow drying, so heated, ventilated spaces help keep projects on schedule.
